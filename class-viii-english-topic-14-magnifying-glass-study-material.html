Topic 14: Magnifying Glass

Unit: Science and Curiosity • Study Material

1. Topic at a Glance

Central idea: A magnifying glass changes the way we observe small things. The poem presents ordinary details of nature as wonderful, complex and almost magical when seen closely.

The lesson also connects observation with science, lenses, visual aids, telescopes, astronomy and the contributions of Indian scholars and scientists.

Key idea to remember: Careful observation can reveal details that are invisible or difficult to notice with the naked eye.

2. The Poem – “Magnifying Glass”

The poem by Walter de la Mare imagines the magnifying glass as something almost magical. Small objects appear much larger and richer in detail.

  • A tiny piece of chalk is shown as containing many shell-like details.
  • A small patch of moss is compared with a forest of flowers and trees.
  • A drop of water is imagined as resembling a hive full of bees.
  • The spider becomes fascinating when its web, spinnerets, claws and many eyes are observed closely.
  • At the end, the power of a lens is extended from tiny objects to the moon, making the distant seem almost reachable.

Poetic effect: The poem creates visual imagery and a feeling of wonder and curiosity.

3. Poem – Important Ideas

IdeaExplanation
ObservationThe speaker looks closely at things that are normally overlooked.
Wonder in natureMagnification makes ordinary natural objects appear extraordinary.
ImaginationThe poet uses imaginative comparisons such as a small patch of moss becoming a forest.
Science and lensesThe final reference to the moon shows how lenses can help us observe distant objects.
CuriosityThe speaker is fascinated by what careful observation reveals.

4. Poetic Devices

  • Simile: The drop of water is compared to a hive of bees using “like”.
  • Personification: The magnifying glass is described as if it can make “Magic talk”.
  • Metaphorical imagery: The tiny patch of moss is presented as a forest of flowers and trees.
  • Alliteration: Repeated beginning sounds add rhythm and musical effect in phrases from the poem.
  • Visual imagery: Shells, moss, flowers, trees, spider, web and moon create strong pictures in the reader’s mind.

6. Language Focus

Collective nouns

The lesson uses expressions such as a hive of bees. Other examples in the exercise include a swarm of locusts, a constellation of stars, a grove of trees, a troupe of dancers, a battalion of soldiers and a fleet of ships.

Idioms with “eye”

  • apple of one’s eye – a very precious person or thing
  • in the blink of an eye – very quickly
  • keep an eye on – watch carefully
  • turn a blind eye – deliberately ignore something
  • see eye to eye – agree with someone
  • bird’s-eye view – a general view from above

Suffix –ish

The lesson draws attention to tigerish, formed by adding -ish to tiger. The suffix can form adjectives from suitable nouns.

7. Pronunciation – /f/ and /v/

The lesson contrasts the sounds /f/ and /v/. The /v/ sound involves vibration, while /f/ does not.

SoundExamples from the lesson

/v/vase, valour, victory, woven, even, marvellous, hive

8. Speaking and Writing

The lesson asks learners to imagine travelling to space and to speak about a planet they would like to visit, preparations, and five things they would take.

It also asks learners to imagine an invention and explain its purpose. In writing, students create a dialogue between Deepa and Asma about making a model for a Science Fair.

Good dialogue writing: mention the setting, use an appropriate tone, include useful information, show agreement/disagreement where relevant, and use bracketed words to show actions or emotions.

9. Visual Aids and Telescopes

The lesson introduces several visual aids for people with low vision:

  • Magnifying spectacles – useful for close-up tasks such as reading or threading a needle.
  • Stand magnifiers – rest above an object and keep the lens at a suitable distance.
  • Hand-held magnifiers – can be moved over printed material; some have built-in lights.
  • Telescopes – help people see distant objects or signs; some can be attached to eyeglasses.

The lesson also explains that telescopes use lenses and introduces astronomical observatories and telescopes, including the Indian Astronomical Observatory, the James Webb Space Telescope and the Mauna Kea Observatory.

Dr. Kailash Chandra SahooThe lesson presents him as a twenty-first-century astronomer popularly known as “the planet hunter”. It describes his work involving stars, black holes, exoplanets and stellar populations.
